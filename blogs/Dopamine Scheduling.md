# Dopamine. That’s Why!

The first thing that comes to mind when we hear “dopamine” is “the happiness chemical” or “the pleasure hormone.” But you’re missing a crucial part: dopamine plays an important role in motivating you to take action and in how intensely you do so.

So, by controlling dopamine, you could actually control your motivation. Yes, I know that sounds even more complicated, and you might be thinking, “How? Is there a valve somewhere in my body that I can just turn to flood myself with dopamine?” No, actually.

But controlling dopamine is easier than you think. Let’s begin with how dopamine works. Your brain constantly releases dopamine; this is called tonic release. It means there is a constant minimum supply of dopamine. That’s what keeps you motivated to do things.

When you anticipate a reward from doing something, neurons in your brain fire and release dopamine above the baseline amount. This is called phasic release, or a spike above the baseline.

Each spike is followed by a dip. A dip can also occur if you don’t get the expected reward.

Your brain learns these patterns and correlates them with whatever task or action you were doing. That’s what teaches you that one thing gives more reward than another.

***It is critically important to maintain sufficient levels of baseline dopamine to sustain day-to-day motivation.***

Imagine a scenario where you’re watching Reels. You opened Instagram out of an urge. That urge comes from dopamine—more precisely, because your brain is expecting a reward and therefore floods your brain with dopamine.

You start watching a reel and get the reward you were expecting. If you don’t get a reward within 3–4 seconds, you scroll in expectation of a reward from the next reel. This cycle continues for hours. It teaches your brain to expect rewards more quickly, making longer, slower tasks like studying and attending lectures feel terribly boring. This also makes some parts of your brain less sensitive to dopamine and other parts that affect anxiety more sensitive.

Most of us have probably heard of a dopamine detox, which means depriving your brain of dopamine to reset this mess.

I suggest a less harsh approach: cleaning your system instead of factory-resetting it.

But there’s more to it: dopamine also greatly influences learning.

During phasic release, a subject’s ability to learn is enhanced, and they can remember more. The opposite happens when dopamine levels drop.

So, improving dopamine levels during study is crucial, while making sure you stay motivated throughout your study session. To achieve this, we need to associate reward with the “struggle” or the “process” and avoid high-frequency, highly stimulating activities before studying.

Meditation helps tremendously. Avoiding doomscrolling early in the morning and anytime before such “boring” tasks is essential.

You may also find yourself less likely to avoid taking other actions, such as helping someone who asks you to, or doing other things where you weigh the effort against the reward. That’s because dopamine also tilts the Go vs. No-Go balance in the basal ganglia.

There are two receptors: D1, which promotes Go, and D2, which suppresses Go and promotes No-Go.

**Dopamine boosts Go signaling and dampens No-Go signaling**
